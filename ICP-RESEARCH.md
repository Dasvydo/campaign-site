## 1. What we may and may not say

### The product, stated exactly

An AI that sits in Outlook. It reads each inbound email, and where the firm's own
documents answer it, writes a reply in that particular person's voice. A human reads it,
changes what they want, and presses send.

Six things about it are verified and safe to state flatly:

1. **It never auto-sends.** There is no automatic send anywhere in the product.
2. **It stays in Outlook.** Drafts appear in the mailbox they already use.
3. **It is hosted in the EU.**
4. **It answers from your own documents.**
5. **It writes in each person's own voice**, built from that person's sent mail.
6. **It asks you a question when the answer is not on file**, instead of inventing one.

Those six are the entire product story. They are also, taken together, a more
differentiated story than anything the competition leads with, which is section 8.

### The one number we measured

**MEASURED: 230 inbound emails produced 35 drafts. That is 15.2%, about one in seven.**

This is the most important number in the campaign and the most commonly misunderstood.
It is not a weakness to hide. It is the honesty that makes the rest believable. The
product does not answer your inbox. It clears the one email in seven that is the same
question you already answered last week, and leaves the other six alone.

Two things about that figure have **not** been measured, and no video may imply
otherwise:

- How often a draft still needs correcting before it goes out.
- The four minutes saved per draft. That figure is **assumed and never timed.** It
  multiplies every euro in the value model, so it is the single largest piece of
  unverified arithmetic in the business.

### Blocked claims: do not put these in anything

| Claim | Why it is blocked |
|---|---|
| Any competitor's price, by name | Sourced from round-ups, never verified against a vendor page |
| "Rivals charge per seat" | Same problem |
| Any hours-saved figure, in any ad | Unverified, and a public measurable promise with nothing behind it |
| Any percentage, in any ad | Unverified |
| "Trusted by N firms", logos, testimonials, named pilots | There are zero closed customers |

The savings argument is still available, but **only as arithmetic the viewer drives with
their own inputs.** The landing page has a calculator for exactly this reason. A video
may show somebody typing their own numbers into it. A video may not say "firms save 51
hours a month."

### The one value line allowed flat

> It pays for itself if the people answering your email cost more than about €5 an hour.

This is legal because it is arithmetic on our own price against the viewer's own volume,
not a claim about our performance. It is also the single best line the campaign owns.
Use it.

### Copy constraints

No em dashes. No "unlock", "supercharge", "seamless", or any AI-brochure register. The
price never appears without its reason. Danish stays natively Danish, Lithuanian stays
in the formal *Jūs* register, and both still need a native speaker's pass before they
meet paid traffic.

### The sentence to keep

> We have no customers to point at yet.

Every instinct says delete it. Keep it. It explains the price, pre-empts the first
objection, and makes the founding-cohort trade coherent instead of looking like a
discount nobody can explain. It is the most credible sentence on the page and it should
appear in the video work too.

## 2. Desk one: property administration

### 2.1 The honest starting point

**There is no independent time-and-motion study of property-manager email volume. Not in
Denmark, not in Lithuania, not anywhere.** Not from NARPM, IREM, Propertymark or
EjendomDanmark. Anyone quoting one is quoting vendor marketing.

That sounds like bad news. It is not. Three other bodies of evidence are stronger than a
time study would be, and they are what the content should stand on:

1. **Regulators say communication is the complaint**, not the underlying problem.
2. **Independent HR data says the role is burning people out**, measurably.
3. **Practitioners self-report their volume in public, in specific numbers**, with names
   attached.

### 2.2 What the inbox actually looks like

From practitioners, in their own words. Use these as **quotes, never as statistics.**

A commercial property manager with about 250 tenants, asked directly how many emails a
day:

> "70-100 per day. Probably about 60% are actionable."

The same person, on coming back from a week off:

> "I have 430 unread emails in my inbox AFTER deleting all the junk."

And on what that does to a life:

> "I have banked like 350 hours of PTO because I'll literally have a thousand emails to
> catch up on when I get back."

A residential manager in the same thread: *"between my PM email and the property email
average 100 per day, with 70% actionable."*

A maintenance coordinator:

> "I sit down, haven't even touched my coffee yet, and I've already got 14 new maintenance
> requests. 14. So the A/C with the baby? I didn't even see that one until like 2pm
> because I was dealing with the first 6 things in my inbox."

**In Denmark the job is literally defined as inbox work.** Newsec's job advert for a
*Foreningsadministrator* lists as a core duty: *"Besvarelse af beboerhenvendelser, på mail
og telefon"* - answering resident enquiries by mail and phone - and describes the role as
*"bindeled"*, the link, between the firm, boards, residents and suppliers. That is the
product's job description, written by the buyer.

### 2.3 The FAQ page is a confession

This is the best research technique in this whole document and the content creator should
use it directly.

**A firm publishes a resident FAQ precisely because it is tired of answering the question
by hand.** So an FAQ page is a public confession of repetitive inbox volume, and every
entry proves the answer already sits in a document.

Dansk Administrationscenter's resident FAQ (dacas.dk/beboer), verbatim Danish, includes:

- *"Hvornår får jeg min nøgle?"* (when do I get my key)
- *"Hvor finder jeg min husorden?"* (where do I find my house rules)
- *"Hvad gør jeg, hvis min nabo larmer?"* (what do I do if my neighbour is noisy)
- *"Hvornår modtager jeg min flytteopgørelse?"* (when do I get my move-out statement)
- *"Hvornår er en skade uopsættelig, og hvornår kan den vente?"* (when is damage urgent)

Civinity Namai's Lithuanian FAQ runs to **17 categories**, including a whole section on
the heating season (*"Kodėl nešyla radiatoriai?"*), one on debts and interest
(*"Atlikau mokėjimą, tačiau sąskaitoje nurodyta skola"* - I paid but the invoice still
shows a debt), and one on document requests (*"Reikalinga pažyma dėl buto pardavimo"* - I
need a certificate for the flat sale).

A Danish administrator's own trade guide summarises the inbound as: *"opkrævninger og
restance. Så er der almindelige spørgsmål om husordenen og vedtægterne samt spørgsmål i
forbindelse med salg."* Collections and arrears, then ordinary questions about the house
rules and the bylaws, plus questions around sales.

**That sentence is the product thesis, stated by the market.** The recurring questions are
answered by two documents plus the file on the unit.

**For the creator:** before making anything, open the FAQ page of five target firms. That
is the message taxonomy, in their own language, for free.

### 2.5 The chase is a loop the firm is funding

Nobody has measured what share of inbound is somebody chasing a reply they are already
owed. But the regulators are unambiguous about what it costs.

**The Property Ombudsman (UK), 2025 annual report**, roughly 24,000 enquiries and 7,681
disputes resolved, in its own words:

> "Poor communication remains the most common underlying issue in complaints. Whether it
> is unclear advice, missed updates or a failure to respond altogether."

> "Many disputes are not driven solely by the original issue. Instead, they escalate
> because expectations were not managed, updates not provided or key information not
> shared at the right time."

**In Lithuania the regulator has documented it.** The Seimas Ombudsman handled 106
resident complaints in 2023 to 2024 about apartment-building administration; 15 were found
justified in 2024. The recorded themes include *"Nereagavimas į gyventojų nusiskundimus"*
- failure to respond to residents' complaints. Residents publish the experience:

> "Į mūsų skambučius, laiškus, prašymus ir skundus nėra reaguojama jau ilgą laiką."
> ("Our calls, letters, requests and complaints have gone unanswered for a long time.")

> "Paprašius pagrįsti sąskaitoje nurodytas sumas, administratorius to nepadaro."
> ("When asked to justify the amounts on the invoice, the administrator does not.")

**And it is what loses the client.** Buildium's 2026 rental owners' survey (n=300,
vendor-published with disclosed method) found the top reasons owners switch management
company:

- **Poor communication, 57%** - the number one reason
- Service quality decline, 54%
- Lack of transparency, 34%

> The thing that loses the client is not the repair. It is the silence about the repair.

**Critical nuance for the creative.** An auto-acknowledgement makes this *worse*. From an
owner whose tenants went over the manager's head:

> "Could they not have said more than 'Acknowledged'?? I'm a renter myself and that's not
> really an answer I'd like when I'm noticing cracks in the ceiling and water in the
> basement."

The product is not a faster autoresponder. It is a real, correct, in-your-voice answer in
five minutes. Say that explicitly, because the segment has been sold the other thing.

### 2.6 Their vocabulary: drowning, fires, guilt

The dominant metaphor is **drowning**. Then **fires**. Then **failure**. Not "efficiency",
not "productivity". The creative should use their words, not ours.

> "Anyone Else Drowning in Emails?" (an actual thread title)

> "Every day I'm drowning in a sea of urgent repair, legal and maintenance emails."

> "Inboxes overflowing. Tenants furious. Owners disappearing until there's someone to blame."

> "The to-do list is a hydra, cut off one head and three more grow. I stopped measuring my
> day by what's left and started measuring by what caught fire that I actually put out.
> Some days just not burning the building down is the win."

> "I might have done 30 things but 300 are outstanding. I feel like a failure at the end of
> every workday."

> "I used to spend a lot of my time and effort soothing ruffled tenant feelings and it was
> exhausting."

**The hard numbers behind the feelings**, and these are independent, not vendor:

- **29.2% total turnover** for onsite property staff over twelve months, **23.4%
  voluntary**, against **14.2%** for corporate roles inside the same companies. (2025 RCLCO
  National Real Estate Compensation and Benefits Survey, via NAA.)
- **50% of onsite leasing professionals** and **46% of onsite management staff** reported
  at least some psychological distress on the Kessler-10 instrument. Both groups worked
  beyond scheduled hours **more than eight times a month** and took **1.4 vacation days**
  in the prior four weeks. (NAA 2024 Mental and Emotional Health Survey.)

**29.2% against 14.2%, inside the same companies, is the number that sells this to an
owner.** It says the churn is not the industry. It is the front-line role, and the
front-line role is the inbox.

### 2.7 The quote that is the product

A residential property manager at roughly 100 emails a day, asked whether her emails are
unique or repetitive:

> "The repetitive ones I have developed templates for over the past 3 years. I use AI to
> respond 99% of the time for the rest, I give it the gist of what I need to say, and let
> it write it. It is critical in this industry that the tone remains the same regardless
> of what I am responding to."

**She has built DoviLoop by hand, over three years, including the voice-consistency
requirement.** This is the single strongest proof point in the property research. It is
not a testimonial, it is better: an unprompted description of the product by someone who
has never heard of it.

And from the same community, the shape of the want:

> "One thing I'd probably change before trying to become even more productive is not
> letting the inbox become the task manager."

### 2.8 Trigger events, and the Danish sales calendar

**Trigger 1: losing an administrator you cannot replace.** EjendomDanmark projects the
Danish property sector needs **up to 10,000 additional employees by 2030, about 9% of the
sector's total employment.** Their chief consultant for education and recruitment:
*"udfordringen med manglende arbejdskraft består"* - the labour shortage challenge
persists. In the UK, property management is named the hardest specialist role to fill.

**Trigger 2: a board threatening to switch administrator.** Newsec publishes a guide on
when an andelsboligforening should change administrator. The **first warning sign listed**:

> "Langsomme reaktioner på henvendelser fra bestyrelse eller andelshavere kan være et tegn
> på manglende prioritering."
> ("Slow responses to enquiries from the board or members can be a sign of lacking
> prioritisation.")

**The competitor's own churn checklist names your product's exact benefit as warning sign
number one.** That is as close to a written buying trigger as research gets.

**This defines the Danish sales calendar.** Administration agreements carry 3 to 6 months'
notice, and a change of administrator is approved at the **generalforsamling**. So: the
churn decision is made at the AGM, and the case for it is built in the weeks before. A
firm just put on notice by a board is in-market. A firm three months after a clean AGM is
not.

**Trigger 3: taking on a portfolio without adding headcount.** **Trigger 4: a regulatory
or complaint shock** - UK ombudsman complaints rose **58% year on year** in Nov 2025 to
Feb 2026 after the Renters' Rights Act. **Trigger 5: a formal case landing**, which turns
every answer into something that must be defensible and on file.

### 2.9 Seasonality: three date-certain Danish waves

**Wave 1, generalforsamling and annual accounts, February to May.** Standard vedtægter
require the ordinary AGM within four months of the financial year end, with notice 14 days
in advance and the accounts sent at the same time. For an administrator with 60
calendar-year foreninger that is 60 AGMs, 60 sets of accounts, 60 notice mailings and 60
sets of minutes inside about ten weeks, plus every member question the accounts provoke.

**It is simultaneously the worst inbox window of the year and the window in which boards
decide whether to keep you.**

**Wave 2, varmeregnskab, June to September.** The heating accounting year normally runs
1 June to 31 May, and the administrator has four months after it ends to deliver the
consumption statement. **Miss the deadline and the landlord loses the right to claim the
balancing payment.** A hard legal deadline with financial forfeiture attached, landing
around 30 September, followed immediately by a wave of "why is my bill this much?" - every
one of which is answered by the statement already sent.

**Wave 3, flyttedage, the 1st of every month, peaking in summer.** Danish tenancies
overwhelmingly start and end on the 1st. Inspection, keys, meter readings, final
settlement and deposit all cluster there.

**Lithuania: the heating season dominates.** It begins **1 October** nationwide. Heating
is expected to cost **at least 20% more** this season, and in 2025-26 an average of about
**164,600 people a month** received heating compensation. October to December is the peak:
heating starts, the first heated-month invoice lands in November with a 20%+ increase, and
every one of those invoices is a potential "justify this" email. On top of that sits a
**monthly** meter-reading declaration cycle.

### 2.10 The objection that will actually come up

**Cost is not the objection. Trust is.** From the First American / DealGround CRE Pulse
Check, May 2026, n=255 commercial real estate professionals:

- **66% use AI weekly or daily** (75.9% at VP level and above). The "they don't use AI"
  premise is dead.
- **Only 5% fully trust AI** for real estate decisions.
- **Only 5% named cost or unclear ROI** as the primary barrier.
- **34% don't know which tools to use. 32% cite concerns about output accuracy.**
- More than half use AI for **workflow assistance only**; **17% apply heavy verification**
  before using AI output.

**Read that as a product specification.** "Workflow assistance only" plus "heavy
verification" plus "5% fully trust" *is* the never-auto-send, human-reviews-and-sends
design. You are not asking this market to trust AI. You are selling the shape of product it
already told a survey it wants. **Lead with the human gate. Do not bury it as a feature.**

**The specific trust problem, and it is vivid.** A property manager's mental model of "AI
writing property emails" is the garbage arriving *from their tenants*:

> "The emails always have city codes or legal document codes. If you look said codes up,
> they're almost always out of context and not even remotely close to what the tenants
> think they are... I just feel like I'm arguing with robots all day."

**You must break that association in the first minute of any video.** The answers:
grounded in *their* documents rather than the open web, shows which document it drew from,
never sends, and sounds like the person rather than like ChatGPT.

**And they have been burned by automation already:**

> "AppFolio has been pushing all these 'new upgrades,' automated text/email workflows, AI
> follow-ups, the whole efficiency sales pitch... yet we're spending MORE time backtracking
> and trying to chase down prospects who literally never saw our messages in the first
> place. It feels like the exact opposite of efficient."

Position explicitly as the corrective to that, not as more of it.

**One more Danish framing, and it is the sharpest available.** An administrator's trade
guide describes the role:

> "Vi kan være den, der skriver rundt, så det ikke begynder at blive personligt."
> ("We can be the one who writes round, so that it doesn't start getting personal.")

The administrator is **paid to be the calm voice**. The pitch is not that AI replaces the
voice. It is that AI gets the calm voice out the door in five minutes instead of five days.
Voice-matching is not a nicety here. It is the licence to operate.

### 2.11 What the stack tells you

Danish property systems (Unik Bolig, EG Bolig, and the 17 listed on businesswith.dk) are
**ledger-and-portal systems. None of them owns the inbox.** So DoviLoop is not competing
with the incumbent system; it fills the gap the incumbent leaves. **Say that to buyers
explicitly, because it removes the rip-and-replace fear.**

Software fatigue is real and organised: r/PropertyManagement has a standing moderator rule
against software solicitation because *"it is constant and drowns out the relevant
conversations."* In the UK, **55% of agents say their systems either add complexity or make
little difference**, only 17% run one main system, and 17% run on email and spreadsheets.

**"No new system, it lives in the Outlook you already have" is not a convenience claim. It
is the objection handler.**

And on shared mailboxes, verified by direct inspection: **ØENS Ejendomsadministration,
serving 300+ Copenhagen associations, publishes exactly one email address: `info@oadv.dk`.**
No individual addresses at all. The front door is a shared mailbox; the work is done by a
named administrator. See section 8.2 - this is the blocking product question.

## 3. Desk two: accounting, bookkeeping and tax

### 3.1 The finding that changes the pitch

**75% of accounting professionals already use AI to draft client emails.** It is the
single most common AI use case in the profession, ahead of summarising documents (71%)
and technical research (69%). By contrast, bank reconciliation is 24% and tax return prep
is 9%.

(Financial Cents, *State of AI in Bookkeeping and Accounting 2026*, 486 North American
professionals, fielded 15 July to 7 August 2026, 68% at 2-30 person firms, 68%
owners or partners. Corroborated directionally by Karbon's own 2025 survey at 63%.)

**You are not selling a new behaviour. You are selling the governed version of a behaviour
they have already adopted ungoverned.**

Because here is the other half: **87% of AI-using firms have no written AI policy.** 15%
have no process at all for handling client data in AI tools. Only 9% require vendor
controls like SOC 2 or no-training clauses.

> The partner's real fear is not that staff will start using AI. It is that they already
> are, in a public tool, with client data, with no policy and no record.

That is the wedge. Shadow AI is the problem; a governed, EU-hosted, document-grounded,
never-sends tool is the answer.

### 3.2 Where the line is, and it is drawn exactly where the product sits

From the same survey:

- Only **19% trust AI enough to use with limited review.**
- **52% actively disagree** that they would trust AI without close oversight.
- **90% agree human judgment matters more, not less**, as AI spreads.

The report's own conclusion: human review is treated as a **permanent design requirement,
not a temporary guardrail firms will eventually remove.**

Two verbatim quotes from respondents:

> "Just because it confidently did the task correctly one time doesn't mean it won't do it
> confidently wrong next."

> "The liability always stays with the human."

**Never-auto-sends is the majority position, not a compromise.**

### 3.3 The inbox

**A caution first.** The number that circulates everywhere in this category - "accountants
handle 100-200 emails a day, which equates to 24 hours a week in their inbox" - is
Karbon marketing with **no methodology at all**: no sample, no survey description, no
telemetry disclosure, no date. Do not use it.

**What is measured and usable:**

**Client communication takes 9.3 hours a week on average. Practitioners say they want it
down to 7.2.** They are self-reporting roughly two hours a week of client communication
they actively want to eliminate. Email is **35.4%** of that time, the largest single
channel. (CPA Practice Advisor / Canopy, 240 accounting professionals, 2024.)

**64% expect email to remain the most-used client channel. Only 37% predict increased
portal use.** After a decade of portal investment, practitioners themselves do not believe
the portal wins. **The product accepts that reality instead of fighting it, which is the
opposite of every incumbent's bet.**

And the single best stat for the "file on that client" feature:

> **91% of accountants regularly want to reference client messages within firm
> communications, but only 42% are sure they can do it easily.**
> (Canopy, 150 US accountants, ±6% at 95% confidence, independent research firm.)

Also from that survey: **79% aren't sure their firm could function without email.** 37%
have missed a client deadline because of poor internal communication. 41% have lost
important client information the same way.

### 3.4 The number one problem is chasing

**"Chasing clients for information" is the number one workflow challenge, named by 64% of
firm owners** - ahead of manual administrative tasks (54.7%), onboarding (35.3%) and poor
work visibility (33.1%). (Financial Cents, *State of Accounting Workflow and Automation
2025*, 816 firm owners.)

Their words, taken directly from that report:

> "I was spending so much time sending emails and tracking things, and not getting the
> paid work done!"

> "Client reminder emails were manual and exhausting. Most of my time was spent chasing,
> not working."

> "Clients didn't respond until I personally emailed them, which was unsustainable."

> "I dropped the ball on a few clients, or ended up doing things after the fact. It was
> embarrassing."

> "When I realized I was spending time away from my toddler doing admin work. Admin work!"

**"Chasing, not working" is the best headline construct in this vertical.** It is their own
antonym pair, and it names the problem exactly.

**The "quick question" genre deserves its own note.** AccountingWEB has a long-running
thread on clients seeking advice by email *"often beginning with 'just a quick email to

> "When your client calls with 'just a quick question,' it's easy to give them an answer
> there and then, but when you do this, you're giving away your expertise for free."

**And there is money attached.** YouGov fieldwork commissioned by Ignition, 506
decision-makers at 1-50 employee US firms: out-of-scope work costs firms **$76,636 a
year** on average. **88% admit delaying or avoiding awkward client conversations.** In the
UK version (470 firms): **£69,957 a year**, and 33% have written off an invoice purely to
dodge the conversation.

### 3.6 Denmark: the strongest Nordic evidence in this whole document

FSR - danske revisorer built these from **Danmarks Statistik register data** (UDDA and
LONN) with Epinion, on graduate cohorts 2010-2020. This is register data, not a survey.

- Total annual hours in year one after graduation: **1,969 in the audit profession against
  1,845 outside it.**
- Among those doing overtime in year one: **296 overtime hours a year, 5.9 a week**,
  against **123 hours, 2.3 a week**, outside the profession.
- Still elevated at ten years: **222 hours a year against 91.**

**And they leave.** From FSR's attrition analysis on the same register data: of the 2010
cohort, 70% were still in the profession at year one, 65% at year two, and **31% at year
ten.** For the 2019 cohort, only **61% made it past year one** - a nine point decline in a
decade.

**Seven out of ten Danish audit firms have problems attracting approved auditors.**
(FSR / Epinion, 2023, n=959 people with cand.merc.aud. background plus 134 audit firms.)

Lone Strøm, then FSR's CEO:

> "Revisorbranchen er en af de bærende søjler i vores tillidssamfund. Derfor er det et
> samfundsproblem, når branchen mangler arbejdskraft."
> ("The audit profession is one of the load-bearing pillars of our society of trust. So it
> is a societal problem when the profession lacks manpower.")

### 3.7 The capacity argument, which is the Lithuanian pitch

**73% of accounting firms are turning away potential clients because they lack staff.**
73% report a "severe" business impact from talent shortages. **74% believe sustained
workloads could push people out of the profession**, and over 25% have personally
considered leaving. (Advancetrack *2026 Accounting Talent Index*, roughly 500 firm leaders
across UK, US, Australia and Canada.)

**This is the single most purchase-relevant statistic in the accounting research.** The
buyer already has demand they cannot serve. "More capacity without headcount" is a
**revenue** pitch, not a cost pitch - which is exactly what Lithuania needs, where the
savings arithmetic does not clear.

### 3.8 Seasonality: Denmark is bimodal, Lithuania is relentless

**Denmark** has no single US-style April. It has two walls:

- **The moms cliff.** VAT frequency is turnover-banded: under DKK 5m is semi-annual (due
  **1 March** and **1 September**), DKK 5-50m quarterly, over DKK 50m monthly.
- **The årsrapport wall.** Due to Erhvervsstyrelsen no later than **six months after
  financial year end**, so **30 June** for calendar-year companies.

A Danish 10-40 person practice hits **at least four hard client-facing communication
spikes a year.** That suits a subscription better than a once-a-year US tax product.

A detail worth exploiting: Erhvervsstyrelsen sends årsrapport reminders to the company's
e-Boks **and a copy to a management member's private digital mailbox**. That reliably
generates a wave of "what is this letter?" emails into the practice.

**Lithuania is monthly and relentless**, not seasonally spiky: i.SAF invoice registers by
the **20th**, VAT FR0600 by the **25th**, GPM313 by the **15th**, all of the following
month. Plus a correction rule that generates email by itself: once a filing deadline has
passed, errors must be fixed **within 10 working days**, and VMI notifies discrepancies
through EDS within two working days.

**That argues for a different message in Lithuania.** Not "survive busy season." Instead:
*"every single month, the same forty emails."*

### 3.9 The workflow is already draft-then-review

This is the most important structural finding for the pitch.

The canonical small-firm workflow: a junior receives documents, reconciles, prepares draft
financials and **marks the work ready for review**; a manager or partner opens it, leaves
comments, and either approves or sends it back; only then does it reach the client.

**Draft, review, send is already the firm's native operating rhythm.** And it has already
extended to email - 75% draft client emails with AI today, with a managing partner in the
same report describing *"mandatory verification of every AI-produced figure."*

> The product is a junior who drafts, and never sends.

**The review step is not friction you have to apologise for. It is the exact shape of the
thing they already trust.**

| Barrier | Share |
|---|---|
| Accuracy and trust in outputs | **26%** |
| Data security and confidentiality | **22%** |
| Don't know where to start | 17% |
| No time to learn or implement | 11% |

**Cost is 3%.** Price is not the fight. Trust is.

**38% named AI's "confident wrongness"** - plausible but inaccurate answers - as their
primary worry.

**The professional bodies have written this down**, and the content should know it exists:

- **ACCA** (*AI in the Finance Profession*, August 2023): *"The question of liability in AI

**The cheapest objection-killer available.** 87% of firms have no written AI policy, and

One thing to have an answer for: **51% of firms already prohibit clients from emailing

### 4.8 The "am I covered?" trap

The single biggest objection, and the evidence vindicates the product's design.

**Coverage questions are not answered by email. That practice predates AI by decades.** An
agency principal describing standard practice:

> "anything along the lines of 'what if' or hypothetical coverage questions are only
> answered over the phone or in person. **My agency prints out the question and reply for
> documentation.**"

Look carefully at what that firm does: **answer by phone, then produce a written record.**
Verbal advice, written record.

**That is precisely the shape of the product** - except it can produce the written record
without the printing.

**Why a broker will never let a machine answer a coverage question unreviewed**, in five
steps:

1. **Coverage determination is fact-dependent, not text retrieval.** The Big "I"'s own
   disclaimer template says it: *"Exclusions and limitations can be absolute or conditional
   based on specific circumstances and loss allegations. Policy language dictates
   coverage."* The right answer often depends on facts the broker does not yet have. A
   model answering from the policy document will confidently answer a question a competent
   human would first refuse and interrogate.
2. **Getting it wrong is a named, insured, litigated E&O category** - "advising coverage
   exists when it doesn't".
3. **The email becomes the evidence.**
4. **The profession has already decided**, and this is taught as E&O hygiene.
5. **The regulator agrees.** EIOPA weights AI risk by autonomy and by whether it is
   consumer-facing. A draft-only, human-approved assistant is low on both axes.

**Disclaimers do not solve it.** The Big "I"'s own guidance, *"Disclaiming Disclaimers"*,
warns that narrow issue-specific disclaimers **create** liability by implying everything
else was warranted.

**What this means for the product, and it is a real recommendation:**

Never-auto-sends is necessary but **not sufficient**. A tired account handler at 5pm on a
renewal Friday will approve a fluent, confident, wrong draft. Human-in-the-loop only works
if the human is cued to check the right thing.

**Build a coverage-question classifier with a different output mode.** When the inbound is
an "am I covered" question, do not draft an answer. Draft one of:

- a **holding reply** that acknowledges, sets a time and offers a call, matching the
  documented professional practice;
- an **internal brief for the handler** - here is the relevant wording, here is the limit,
  here is the exclusion, here is what you do not know yet - which the handler uses on the
  phone; then
- a **post-call confirmation draft** writing up what was discussed.

**That third output is the killer feature.** It automates *"my agency prints out the
question and reply for documentation"* - the exact practice a real principal described, and
exactly what Nancy Germond means by *"if you can't document that you discussed it, it's a
problem."*

### 5.1 Cost is not the objection. Trust is.

This reframes everything. The partner's fear is not adoption. It is that adoption already

**And it hands you the cheapest objection-killer in the entire document:** ship a

Financial Cents' own conclusion is the line to remember: human review is treated as **a
permanent design requirement, not a temporary guardrail firms will eventually eliminate.**

> **Never-auto-sends is not a limitation to apologise for. It is the majority position,

Lead with the gate. Do not bury it in a feature list.

### 5.4 Draft-then-review is already how these firms work

Accounting has it explicitly: a junior prepares, marks it ready for review; a manager
comments, approves or returns it; only then does it reach the client.

Insurance has it in the three-tier service model, where work flows **downhill to the

Property has it in the informal version: the administrator drafts, the partner sees

> **The product is a junior who drafts, and never sends.**

That single sentence does more work than any feature list, because it describes something

## 6. The three markets are three different sales, and only one of them is about money

This is the most consequential thing in this document for how the content gets made,
and it comes straight out of the campaign's own pricing work rather than from research.

### 6.1 Denmark: sell the money

A loaded hour costs about **€30** (junior accountant, roughly 33,000 DKK a month gross
plus employer cost, over 160 hours).

At that rate the arithmetic clears comfortably at every volume the product sells into.
A ten person firm where each person takes 500 emails a month is looking at roughly 51
hours handed back, against a fee of €149. Even at the bottom of the modelled range, 200
emails a person, it clears about 4x.

**Denmark is the market where you may point at the number and let it do the work** (as
arithmetic the viewer drives, never as an asserted saving). The calculator is the hero.

### 6.2 Lithuania: do not sell the money, sell the capacity

A loaded hour costs about **€10**.

Run the same sum and a ten person Lithuanian firm at 200 emails a person keeps **€54 a
month**. That is not an offer. A partner will see it in ten seconds and the pitch dies
in the room.

Lithuania needs 300+ emails a person on Firm, or 500 on Desk, before a savings argument
survives contact with arithmetic. Below that the honest pitch is a different one
entirely:

> Answer more without hiring.

Not cheaper. **More.** The same people cover a bigger book, replies go out the same day
instead of Thursday, and the firm takes on the next portfolio without adding a desk.
This is a capacity story and a service-quality story, and it does not depend on the
hourly rate at all.

**This is a targeting decision, not a copy problem.** If the Lithuanian creative leads
with savings it will fail, and it will fail for a reason no amount of editing fixes.

### 6.3 English and global: sell the mechanism

The English route has no single labour cost to anchor on, so it cannot lead with either
country's arithmetic. It leads with what the thing actually does: grounded in your own
documents, in your own voice, and it never sends. The calculator handles the money by
asking the visitor for their own hourly figure.

## 7. Where the money argument breaks, and what to do about it

Worth saying plainly to whoever is making the content, because it will come up.

The value model rests on **four minutes saved per draft, which has never been timed.**
Five minutes to write a reply from nothing, one minute to read a prepared draft and send
it. It is a reasonable assumption. It is still an assumption, and it multiplies every
figure in the model.

At two minutes instead of four, Denmark at 500 emails a person falls from about 10x to
about 5x, and Lithuania stops working almost everywhere.

**INFERENCE:** the highest-value piece of research this business could do this month is
not more market research. It is one week, one real mailbox, a stopwatch, and an honest
count of how long a prepared draft actually takes to check and send versus writing the
same reply cold. That single measurement either earns you the right to state a figure in
an ad, or tells you the Lithuanian market is not reachable at this price. Everything in
section 6 is downstream of it.

Until it is done, the content cannot assert a time saving, and should not try.

## 8. The competitive picture, and the one fact that reorganises the whole campaign

### 8.1 Copilot does not work where your buyers' email actually lives

Microsoft's own Outlook FAQ, verbatim:

> "Copilot scenarios in Outlook are only available on a user's primary mailbox. They are
> not available on a user's archive mailbox, group mailboxes, or shared and delegate
> mailboxes that they have access to."

Property administration runs on `udlejning@` and `info@`. Accounting runs on `kontakt@`.
Insurance broking runs on `skade@` and `service@`. **The shared inbox is the workplace in
all three trades, and Copilot is switched off there.**

Be scrupulous about the workaround, because a competent IT provider will know it. There
is one: the separate Microsoft Copilot app can read and draft in shared and delegate
mailboxes. But it needs a paid Copilot licence per user, full delegate access rather than
folder permissions, and it means leaving Outlook to write the reply. The honest claim,
which survives scrutiny:

> Copilot does not work on your shared inbox inside Outlook. There is a workaround, and it
> means a paid licence for everyone, delegate access configured properly, and writing your
> replies in a different app.

**Three more things Copilot genuinely cannot do**, all documented by Microsoft:

- **It does not learn your voice.** Microsoft's method is that you type instructions
  describing yourself ("keep emails short and direct, sign with my first name"). One
  firm-wide instruction string is not seven people's voices.
- **The feature that does learn voice is Copilot Tuning**, still early access, requiring
  at least 20 sample files plus a written style guide. An admin project, not a setting.
- **The free tier reads the mailbox, not the document library.** Copilot Chat is genuinely
  included free, and it is more capable in Outlook than founders usually assume. But
  Microsoft states plainly that without a paid licence it gets "a limited set of
  organizational content and reduced graph grounding." It cannot reason over the lease,
  the engagement letter, the policy wording, or the file on that client.

**Do not argue Copilot is bad.** Argue that it is general purpose, per seat, mailbox only,
and blind to the shared inbox. All four are Microsoft's own documentation.

### 8.2 THE BLOCKING QUESTION: does DoviLoop work on shared mailboxes?

**Nothing in the repo says.** The word "shared" appears only in reference to pooled
drafts. The pricing counts "mailboxes" and the voice profile is per person, both of
which read as individual-mailbox assumptions.

This is now the most important open question in the campaign, because it cuts both ways:

- **If yes**, the Copilot fact above is the strongest weapon the product has, the flat
  per-firm price becomes an obvious answer to per-mailbox rivals, and there is a clean
  line: *"Microsoft's own documentation says Copilot skips your shared inbox. We start
  there."*
- **If no**, then the product does not work where these three trades actually do their
  email, and the ICP itself needs rethinking before a euro is spent on creative.

Nothing else in this document matters as much. **Answer it before the creator starts.**

A second-order question if the answer is yes: a shared mailbox has no single "voice" to
profile. Whose voice does a draft in `info@` get written in? That is a product decision
with direct copy consequences.

## 9. The objection map

Ordered by how much each one actually matters, using the measured barrier weights from
section 5.1 rather than by how often founders worry about them.

For each: what they say, **what is really being asked**, the answer, and the proof.

---

### 9.1 "It will write something wrong and we will send it." (26% accounting, 32% property)

**Really being asked:** *who carries the liability when it is wrong?*

**The answer, in order:**

1. **It cannot send.** Not "will not by default." Cannot. Every competitor offering
   auto-send has to defend that choice; you never do.
2. **It only drafts what your files answer.** About one in seven. The rest it leaves alone.
3. **When the answer is not on file, it asks you a question** instead of filling the gap
   with something that reads well.
4. **Every factual claim cites the document it came from**, so review is a glance rather
   than a re-read.

**The proof:** 90% of accounting professionals say human judgment matters more, not less,
as AI spreads. Only 19% would use AI with limited review; 52% actively disagree they would.
Only 5% of property professionals fully trust AI, and over half use it for workflow
assistance only.

> **Reframe: your professional body requires human review of AI output. We do not merely
> permit that, we enforce it.** The constraint is a compliance feature.

**Point 4 is not optional in insurance.** Every broker email is discoverable, and a
hastily-approved AI draft becomes Exhibit A. **Citation-to-source is the E&O feature.**

---

### 9.2 "Where does our client data go?" (22% accounting, 24% insurance)

**Really being asked:** *can I show this to my IT provider, my board, or my regulator
without it becoming a problem?*

**The answer:** do not say "GDPR compliant" - everyone says it and it means nothing. Say
the specific things: hosted in the EU, named sub-processors, a DPA available **before**
signature, and your mail is never used to train a model.

**Then go one better than the entire market: publish the sub-processor list and the DPA on
the website, unauthenticated, so the IT provider can self-serve the answer without a sales
call.** Nobody in this landscape does that.

**Why it matters more in Denmark than anywhere:** **Datatilsynet named artificial
intelligence among its 2026 supervisory focus areas**, expecting documentation, risk
assessments and transparency. A Copenhagen property administrator has published its own
explainer on this - **the objection arrives pre-formed.** Have the databehandleraftale, EU
residency statement and a DPIA template ready before the first Danish call.

**The market confirms the objection is real:** all three EU competitors lead with data
residency (*"100% EU Data Residency"*, *"alle data ligger fysisk i EU-datacentre"*, *"data
residency in the EU, full DPA on request"*), while Fyxer, Superhuman, Serif, Front,
Missive, Shortwave, Cora and MailMaestro make **no residency claim at all.**

**Really being asked:** *is this a real product or a wrapper?*

**Do not argue Copilot is bad.** Argue it is general purpose, per seat, mailbox only, and
blind to the shared inbox. All four are Microsoft's own documentation. The full answer is
section 8.1. The short version:

> "You probably already have Copilot Chat, free with your licence. Try it on your shared

> only runs on a user's primary mailbox. And the free tier reads your mailbox, not your
> document library. To get that you are at per-seat pricing, and even then Copilot does not
> know how *you* write. Microsoft's advice is to type instructions describing yourself.
> Keep Copilot for Word and Excel. Use us for the inbox."

**Be scrupulous about the workaround** - a competent IT provider knows the separate Copilot
app can reach shared mailboxes. It needs a paid licence per user, full delegate access, and
leaving Outlook to write the reply. Say that honestly; it still wins.

### 9.4 "We tried ChatGPT and it wrote nonsense that did not sound like us."

**Really being asked:** *prove this is different before I spend another hour on AI.*

**Agree loudly, then explain why it happened:** ChatGPT had no access to their sent mail or
their files, so it had nothing to imitate and nothing to cite.

**Then stop talking and demo it.** This objection is not beaten by argument. It is beaten by
showing a draft of *their* email that sounds like *their* colleague.

**The strongest version of this in property is vivid and specific:** a property manager's
mental model of "AI writing property emails" is **the garbage arriving from their tenants** -

> "The emails always have city codes or legal document codes... they're almost always out
> of context... I just feel like I'm arguing with robots all day."

**Break that association in the first minute.** Grounded in *their* documents rather than
the open web, shows its source, never sends, sounds like the person.

### 9.5 "Our clients would hate this" / "they pay for a human"

**Really being asked:** *will this damage the relationship I sell?*

**The reframe:** you are not adding outbound volume. You are removing the delay before a
human replies.

**The Danish framing is the sharpest available.** An administrator's trade guide describes
the job: *"Vi kan være den, der skriver rundt, så det ikke begynder at blive personligt"* -
we can be the one who writes round, so it does not start getting personal.

> **The administrator is paid to be the calm voice. The pitch is not that AI replaces the
> voice. It is that AI gets the calm voice out the door in five minutes instead of five
> days.** Voice-matching is not a nicety here. It is the licence to operate.

**And the market is moving the other way on this.** In 2026, *"failure to adopt modern
technology and AI tools"* entered the top eight reasons employers switch insurance brokers
for the first time.

---

### 9.6 "Our staff will think they are being replaced."

**Really being asked:** *will this blow up in my face internally?*

**The answer is structural, not reassurance:** the product writes in **each person's own
voice**, and **they** press send. That is amplification of the individual, not replacement.

**Frame as** *"your best writer's standard, on everyone's worst day"* and *"you stop typing,
you keep judging."* **Never** put headcount-reduction arithmetic in sales material for this
segment - it will be forwarded to the staff and kill the deal.

**The champion is usually the person drowning.** The property manager who spent three years
building templates and now uses AI for 99% of the rest is not your obstacle; she is your
advocate, and she already built this by hand.

**A practical proof pattern:** in the pilot, let each person see their own draft-acceptance
rate. People who see their own name on the output stop treating it as a threat.

### 9.7 "Nobody here knows enough to decide" / "we have no AI policy"

**Really being asked:** *I do not want to be the one who authorised something I do not
understand.*

**This is the cheapest objection in the document to kill.** 87% of AI-using accounting
firms have no written AI policy. 55% of insurance agencies have none. Only 29% of agency
principals feel knowledgeable enough to make AI decisions. And every professional body -
ICAEW, ACCA, FSR - says the remedy is: write a policy.

> **Ship a ready-made AI use policy template with the product.** One page, editable, aligned
> to what their body already says. It costs a weekend and removes a named stall in all three
> verticals. **Nobody in this landscape is doing it.**

In insurance, go further: **get an E&O broker or carrier to review the architecture and say
something quotable.** That is the highest-leverage single asset available in that vertical.

### 9.8 "Another system, another integration, our IT cannot touch Outlook."

**Really being asked:** *how much of my time and my IT provider's goodwill does this cost?*

**Lead with the install, not the outcome.** *"It is an Outlook add-in. Nothing moves. If you
hate it, you remove the add-in and everything is exactly as it was."* **Reversibility beats
benefit for risk-averse buyers.**

**The permission model is a gift.** The least-privilege permission for a drafting product is
delegated `Mail.ReadWrite`, which Microsoft documents as explicitly **not including
permission to send mail**:

> "We ask for permission to write drafts. We do not ask for permission to send mail.
> Microsoft's own permission model will not even let us."

**Two things to do before selling:** get **Microsoft Publisher Verification** (many tenants
block unverified publishers outright under the common restrictive consent policy), and
**never request application-level permissions** - those always need a Global Admin and will
frighten an outsourced IT provider. Ship an admin-consent URL plus a one-page PDF listing
each scope and why.

**Software fatigue is real and organised.** 55% of UK letting agents say their systems add
complexity or make little difference. r/PropertyManagement has a moderator rule against
software solicitation. **"No new system" is not a convenience claim. It is the objection
handler.**

---

### 9.9 "Our emails are too specific to be automated."

**Really being asked, usually:** *my knowledge is valuable and you are about to tell me it
is not.*

**This is pride, not a technical objection, and it is the only one on this list that is
secretly a buying signal.** Never argue. Invert it:

> "You are right. That is exactly why generic AI failed you. It did not have your files."

**Then run the proof:** ask for twenty real redacted inbound emails, draft replies from
their own documents, and show them side by side with what they actually sent.

**This converts the objection into the demo**, and it is also the single most persuasive
content format available (section 12).

### 9.10 "How much?" - and why it comes last

**Cost is 3% of stated barriers in accounting and 5% in property.** It is not the fight.

When it does come, the answer is the flat fee against a market that is universally per-seat,
per-mailbox or credit-metered, plus the structure: no card, month to month, nothing invoiced
until day 14, and 150 usable drafts in 30 days or the month is free.

**And the price never appears without its reason.** Not "€149 a month." Instead: *"€149 a
month for the first five firms, because we need five firms who will say it works."*

**Keep "we have no customers to point at yet."** It explains the price, pre-empts the
objection, and makes the trade coherent instead of looking like a discount nobody can
explain.

### 10.2 The legal fact that decides the outbound motion

**Denmark: cold email is effectively closed.**

Markedsføringsloven § 10, stk. 1, in the statutory text:

> "Erhvervsdrivende må ikke rette henvendelse til **nogen** ved brug af elektronisk post...
> med henblik på direkte markedsføring, medmindre modtageren har givet sit forudgående
> samtykke til det."

The word is **"nogen"** - *anyone*. Legal commentary is consistent that the prohibition
applies whether the recipient is a consumer or a business. There is a catch-22 in it: you
generally **cannot email to ask for consent**, because the request is itself marketing.

Enforcement is real. Verified fines from Forbrugerombudsmanden: a standard first-offence
administrative fine of **DKK 10,000**; **DKK 20,000** accepted for unsolicited marketing to
a single consumer; **DKK 964,600** for unsolicited approaches to citizens' e-Boks; and
**DKK 800,000** for a course company, where the court calculated from **DKK 100 per email
sent**.

**⚠️ Confirm this with Danish counsel before sending anything.** The Consumer Ombudsman's
own guidance PDF could not be extracted, its spam page returned 404, and the government's
business portal discusses only consumers. **The B2B-applies reading rests on the statutory
wording plus law-firm commentary.** That is strong, but it is not advice, and the campaign
code already carries an `outreach` attribution source - so somebody is planning to send.

**Lithuania: cold email just opened up.**

Effective **22 April 2026**, Article 81 of the Lithuanian Law on Electronic Communications
was amended to create a **direct-marketing exception for legal entities.** Prior consent is
no longer required to market to a legal entity, covering both generic addresses
(`info@company.lt`) and **named employee addresses**.

Conditions that still bind: a clear, free and easy opt-out in every message; marketing
stops without undue delay on objection; the DPA recommends keeping records of objections;
and **GDPR still applies separately** to a named employee's personal data, normally on
legitimate interest.

**⚠️ Also needs counsel.** The DPA's own announcement page returned 403; this is
reconstructed from consistent secondary sources citing it, plus the law text on infolex.lt.

> **Your two EU markets now require opposite motions. Denmark: consent-first, inbound,
> events, paid social, phone. Lithuania: compliant B2B cold email is available.**
> **If outbound is a channel you want, build it in Lithuania first.** Porting that playbook
> to Denmark would be a fineable mistake.

Paid social is unaffected by the spam ban: **running Meta ads to a Danish audience is
fine.** What does apply is general marketing law (truthful claims, disclosure of
advertising) and ePrivacy consent for the Meta Pixel, which the page already gates behind
its cookie banner.

### 10.5 What content format actually works

The honest finding: **almost every B2B video "benchmark" available is published by an
agency selling that exact service, and at least one pair of them directly contradicts each
other** (one 2026 LinkedIn benchmark claims single-image ads out-click video 0.42% to
0.24%; others claim video drives 34% higher CTR). **Do not let the creator build a plan
that depends on either.** Test both. You will have your own data in two weeks and it will
be worth more than all of it.

The one near-primary source is **Wistia's State of Video** (~1,000 professionals surveyed,
13M+ videos, 79M hours of viewing data):

- Videos **under one minute average a 52% engagement rate** but only about **1% CTR**.
  Long-form (30-60 min) gets **23% CTR**.
- **Educational content has the highest engagement of any video type**, consistently
  beating product demos, testimonials and promos at every length.
- **LinkedIn is B2B's primary video channel** - 8 in 10 teams say so.

> **Short-form buys attention. Long-form converts.** Do not ask a 30-second video to close.
> Use short hooks on Meta and LinkedIn pointing at a real demo on the landing page.

The qualitative finding is consistent across every source: **founder-led beats polished
production for B2B**, because the best content *"feels like advice from a smart colleague,
not a pitch from a vendor."*

**And language matters more than format.** EchoYou and WALLY are both advertising in
Danish. English creative into Denmark will underperform against localised competitors.

### 11.4 The rule this suggests

**For this audience, a specific scene beats a statistic every time.**

A partner does not recognise themselves in "117 emails a day." They recognise themselves in
a subject line that reads *"Deposit statement, still nothing"*, or in a colleague saying
*"most of my time was spent chasing, not working."*

**Lead with the scene. Keep the statistics for the landing page and the sales call**, where
a sceptic can click the source.

## 12. The creative brief

### 12.1 The test every asset has to pass

> Would a partner screenshot this and send it to a colleague saying "this is us"?

If no, it is a product video, not a marketing video, and it will not work on this audience.

### 12.2 Learn from the ads already running

These are live ads pulled from the Meta Ad Library, not remembered campaigns.

**The one to beat, because it is your positioning in your home market:**
**EchoYou, in Danish: "AI-svar, der lyder som dig"** - *AI replies that sound like you.*
Names the outcome in the buyer's own words with zero jargon.
**Go one level deeper than it can: they say "sounds like you." You can say "sounds like
you, and knows the file on that client."** EchoYou has no document grounding.

**The pain hook, also Danish:** **WALLY: "Er du også træt af at sende mails?"** - *Are you
tired of sending emails too?* Colloquial, phrased as a question, with *også* ("too") doing
the work of making the viewer feel seen. **They are running six creative variants of one
hook. Copy the method, not the line.**

**The best headline in the whole set:** **Superhuman: "Replies, Ready to Send."** Four
words describing the exact moment of relief. Not a feature, a state of the world.

**The "that's literally us" mechanic in its purest form:** **Fireflies: "No one wants 99+
inbox before 9am."** It names **a number you have personally seen on your own screen.** The
specificity of "99+" and "9am" does all the work.

> **This is the template for the property creative: "Nobody wants forty tenant emails
> before the coffee is made."**

**What to avoid:** Property Matrix's *"AI Bill Entry, Online Leasing & Maintenance Tracking
in One Platform."* Feature list, no emotion, no recognition.

**And one to deliberately contradict:** Weblo promises it *"responds to tenants and handles
admin automatically."* Section 5.8 explains why automatic is the wrong promise in this
segment. **Say the opposite, out loud.**

### 12.3 The twelve assets

Every one is legal under the claims gate in section 1. All are buildable with screen
recording plus one person on a phone camera.

---

**A1. "The five sources" - the flagship. 30 seconds. Build this first.**
*Screen recording, voiceover, no face.*

The landing page already contains this mechanic and it is buried three scrolls down. It is
the best thing the product owns.

| Beat | On screen | Voiceover |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Real inbound email. All five toggles OFF. A flat generic draft | "This is what every other tool writes you." |
| 0:07 | Toggle on *the rules* | "Now with your own rules." |
| 0:12 | Toggle on *the deadline*. A date appears | "Your deadline." |
| 0:17 | Toggle on *the file*. The client's name replaces "Dear client" | "The file on that client." |
| 0:22 | Toggle on *the figures*. Three real numbers land | "The figures already logged." |
| 0:26 | Toggle on *the tone*. Wording relaxes into house voice | "And the way your office actually writes." |
| 0:29 | The whole draft | "Same email. Your files. That is the entire product." |

**Why it works:** demonstrates the one thing no competitor has, without asserting a single
blocked claim. Three versions, one per desk, changing only the email and the sources.

---

**A2. "The gate" - the trust film. 12 seconds.**
*Screen recording, text on screen, no voiceover.*

Draft ready. Cursor moves toward Send. **Freeze.** Text: *"There is no send button on our
side. There never was."* Cursor then edits two words, and a human sends it.

**Why it works:** 90% of accountants say human judgment matters more as AI spreads; only
5% of property professionals fully trust AI. This is the majority position in twelve
seconds. Runs standalone and as the last beat of longer cuts.

---

**A3. "It asks you a question" - the unoccupied ground. 20 seconds.**
*Screen recording.*

Show the product **not** drafting. A message arrives; instead of a draft, a question:
*"I do not have the handover date for flat 214. What should I say?"*

Close: *"The one you can let near a client is the one that admits what it does not know."*

**Why it works:** every competitor sells confidence. **38% of accountants named AI's
"confident wrongness" as their primary worry.** Selling the refusal to guess is empty
ground.

---

**A4. "The seventh email" - the honesty hook. 15 seconds.**
*Simple motion graphic.* Seven envelopes, six stay grey, one lights up.

> "We are not going to answer your inbox. About one email in seven is the same question you
> answered last week. That is the one we draft. You keep the other six."

**Why it works:** under-promising is the hook, it inoculates against the week-two
disappointment that kills these products, and 15.2% is measured so it is safe to state.

---

**A5. Three "subject line" films. 10 seconds each.**
*Hold on one subject line, then one line of text.*

- Property: **"Deposit statement, still nothing"**
- Accounting: **"VAT return, are we late?"**
- Insurance: **"Has the adjuster called?"**

Then: *"You know the answer. It is in a document you already have. It has been sitting
there four days because nobody had ten minutes."*

**The subject line is the targeting.** Three cuts, three audiences. The creative does the
segmentation the ad platform cannot.

---

**A6. "Forty before the coffee" - the recognition ad. 15 seconds.**
*Screen recording of a real, anonymised shared inbox at 08:00.*

No voiceover for the first three seconds. Just the scroll. Then one line:
**"Nobody wants forty tenant emails before the coffee is made."**

This is the Fireflies mechanic applied to the vertical. **Name a number they have seen on
their own screen.**

---

**A7. "Chasing, not working" - the accounting cut. 20 seconds.**

Built on the firm owner's own words: *"Most of my time was spent chasing, not working."*
Show the chase sequence - day 0 request, day 3 reminder, day 7 deadline - then the reply
that finally arrives, then the draft already waiting.

**Why it works:** chasing clients for information is the **number one** named workflow
problem at 64%. Their antonym pair, not ours.

---

**A8. "Five places" - founder to camera. 30 seconds.**
*Phone camera. No production. No teleprompter.*

> "There are five places. It is not a marketing five. Our system polls about fifty
> mailboxes at once and that is the actual ceiling. When they are gone I will take the
> counter off the page. I run the setup calls myself, so five is what I can do."

**Why it works:** scarcity that is true and checkable, to an audience that has seen a lot of
theatre. Do not light it nicely.

---

**A9. "What it does not do" - the disqualifier. 35 seconds.**
*Founder to camera.*

> "Under ten people, do not buy this, go use the cheaper plan on the product site. Not on
> Outlook, it works, but it is more setup and we will talk about that. And if your mail is
> all genuine one-off advice with nothing repeating, this saves you nothing. We would
> rather say that on the call than sell it to you."

**Likely the highest-converting asset in the set for a sceptical partner**, because it is
the only one that demonstrates willingness to lose the sale. The landing page already
routes firms under ten people away. The video should too.

---

**A10. "Day 0, Day 14" - the risk reversal. 20 seconds.**
*Three-card motion graphic.*

**Day 0.** One setup call. Drafts start. Nothing invoiced.
**Day 14.** A review call. The first month is invoiced only if you say yes.
**Say no.** Nothing invoiced, nothing to cancel, no card was ever taken.

Plus: 150 usable drafts in the first 30 days, or the month is free.

Against a market of three-month bindings, fifty-seat minimums and credit meters, this is a
real differentiator and it is currently buried in the pricing section.

---

**A11. "Not automatic" - the corrective. 15 seconds.**

Open on the competitor promise: *"handles your tenant emails automatically."* Then:

> "We looked at what happened when firms bought that. They ended up chasing more, not less.
> One property manager said it felt like the exact opposite of efficient.
> So: it drafts. You send. That is the difference."

**Why it works:** directly contradicts a live competitor ad, and it is backed by a real
practitioner quote. **Do not name the competitor** - the claims gate blocks that, and it is
unnecessary.

---

**A12. "The confirming email" - the insurance cut. 25 seconds.** *English only.*

Put Swiss Re's guidance on screen:

> "Whenever a customer requests a change, send a quick email confirmation. It documents your
> action and keeps everyone aligned."
> - Senior Underwriter, Swiss Re Corporate Solutions

Then: *"Your E&O carrier already told you to do this. Nobody has the time. Now it is
drafted before you open the message."*

**This is the strongest single asset available in the insurance vertical** and nobody else
is running it. Position as risk control, not productivity.

### 12.4 Production rules

- **No stock footage of people smiling at laptops.** Screen recordings, real interfaces,
  one real face.
- **Nothing from the blocked list**, including on-screen text, captions and thumbnails. No
  hours saved. No percentages. No competitor names or prices.
- **No em dashes** anywhere on screen.
- **Three desks, three cuts.** The generic version underperforms all three.
- **Match language to landing route.** Danish ads to `/da`, Lithuanian to `/lt`. Both still
  need a native speaker's pass.
- **Subtitle everything.** This audience watches muted, at a desk, between emails.
- **Lead with the scene, not the statistic.** Section 11.4.
- **Swap in real messages as soon as one customer permits it.** Every sender, figure and
  date in the current worked example is invented, which the page admits. Three real
  anonymised threads are the largest single improvement available to the creative.

### 12.5 Sequencing

**Week 1, the spine.** A1 in all three desk versions, and A2. These carry the product
story; everything else is support.

**Week 2, the recognition.** A5, A6, A7. Cheap to make, and they do the audience
segmentation.

**Week 3, the person.** A8, A9, A10. Founder to camera, deliberately unpolished.

**Week 4, the edges.** A3, A11, A12.

## 13. The delegation package

What to actually send the person doing content, demos and AI video, and in what order.

### Send him these four things and nothing else at first

1. **This document, sections 1 and 9.** Section 1 is the claims gate, which is
   non-negotiable and will otherwise cost him remakes. Section 9 is the nine scripts.
2. **The three desk examples** from the landing page copy, so he has the real shape of a
   property, accounting and insurance message.
3. **Access to the live page**, so he can screen-record the five-source toggle and the
   calculator rather than rebuilding them.
4. **One paragraph of brand constraints:** no em dashes, no AI-brochure vocabulary, the
   two brand faces, and the rule that amber appears once per surface as a marker and
   never as a recoloured word in a headline.

### The order to build in

**Week 1, the spine.** A1 (five sources) in all three desk versions, and A2 (the gate).
These two carry the product story and everything else is support. A1 in particular is
reusable as the middle of every longer cut.

**Week 2, the targeting.** The three A5 subject-line films, plus A4 (the seventh email).
Cheap to make, and they are what actually does the audience segmentation.

**Week 3, the person.** A7 (five places) and A8 (what it does not do), both founder to
camera, both deliberately unpolished. Then A9 (Day 0 / Day 14).

**Week 4, the extras.** A3 (it asks you a question) and A6 (08:40 / 08:41).

### What to brief him to avoid

- Any hours-saved number, any percentage, any competitor name, any implication of
  existing customers. If a script needs a number to work, the number goes in the
  calculator on the page and the script points at it.
- Polished corporate production. The audience is a partner in a ten-person firm who has
  been sold to before. Screen recordings and a phone camera read as true; a colour-graded
  brand film reads as a company with more marketing budget than customers, which is
  exactly what we are and exactly what we should not look like.
- Generic "AI for business" framing. The subject line is the targeting. A video that
  could be for any firm reaches no firm.

### How to measure whether it worked

The page already fires eight events with market, locale and full UTM attribution on every
one. That means the creative can be judged properly, by market, if and only if each asset
ships with its own `utm_content`. **Give every cut a distinct `utm_content` value and
write them down somewhere.** Otherwise there will be a pile of traffic and no way to tell
which of the nine did it.

The events worth watching, in order of how much they mean: `demo_desk` (did they play
with the toggle), `pricing_view`, `form_start`, `qualified_shown`, `booking_click`.

**INFERENCE:** `demo_desk` is the leading indicator to optimise against. Someone who
turns the sources off and on has understood the product. That is a much better signal
than a scroll or a click, and it is the thing the flagship asset is designed to cause.

## 14. What we still do not know

A brief that hides its gaps produces content that asserts things nobody checked.

### 14.1 Blocking, in priority order

**1. Does the product work on shared mailboxes?** Section 8.2. It is both the strongest
competitive weapon available and, if the answer is no, a reason to rethink the ICP.
Everything else on this list is secondary. **Answer it before the creator starts.**

**2. The four minutes has never been timed.** One week, one real mailbox, a stopwatch.
It decides whether Lithuania is reachable at this price and whether an hours figure can ever
appear in an ad. At two minutes instead of four, Denmark falls from about 10x to about 5x
and Lithuania stops working almost everywhere.

**3. How often a draft still needs correcting.** Unmeasured, and openly admitted on the
page. It is the first question a careful partner asks. Measure it during the founding cohort
and it becomes the strongest proof point the product has.

**4. Real example messages.** Every sender, figure and date in the current worked example is
invented. Three real anonymised threads, one per desk, cost one customer's permission and
are the largest single improvement available to both the page and the video work.

**5. Emails per day, in any of the three trades.** **No credible independent measurement
exists anywhere, in any of them.** That is a gap in the public record, not a gap in the
research. **Measure it across the first ten installs and you own the industry's reference
number** - in all three verticals. This is a genuine, cheap, ownable asset.

### 14.2 Legal, before anything ships

- **Danish counsel on markedsføringsloven § 10 and B2B cold email.** The statutory wording
  says *nogen* (anyone) and commentary is consistent, but the Consumer Ombudsman's own
  guidance could not be retrieved. The code already carries an `outreach` attribution
  source, so this is urgent rather than theoretical.
- **Lithuanian counsel on the 22 April 2026 Article 81 amendment.** The DPA's announcement
  page returned 403; the finding is reconstructed from consistent secondary sources.
- **Danish cookie-consent enforcement** for the Meta Pixel specifically.
- **An E&O carrier or broker opinion** on AI-assisted drafting with mandatory human review.
  No published insurer document was found stating that reviewed AI drafting triggers an
  exclusion - the exposure the market prices is *autonomous or unreviewed* output. That is
  a defensible position, **but get it in writing before claiming it in marketing.**

### 14.3 Numbers worth confirming before publishing

- **The Danish insurance broker count.** 183 firms with 140 under DKK 5m came via search
  indexing because fmf.dk returned 503. The Danish FSA's public register is authoritative.
  This number decides whether DK insurance is a vertical or an account list.
- **The Lithuanian broker count** (~107). Needs confirming with Lietuvos bankas directly.
- **The Copilot Tuning seat threshold.** Reportedly 5,000 licences for preview eligibility,
  but Microsoft's own FAQ says only "a limited set of customers." **If it is confirmed at
  5,000, it is the best single line in the sales deck:** *"Microsoft's own voice-training
  feature needs 5,000 seats. You have 18."*
- **Superhuman's Outlook support and monthly pricing.** Their plans page does not mention
  Outlook at all; the help centre returned 403.
- **Danish huslejenævn case figures** - came via search summarisation, not a direct read.

### 14.4 Sizing gaps, and how to close them cheaply

Both national statistics offices hold what is missing, behind dashboards rather than pages:

- **Denmark:** Statistics Denmark's Statbank, enterprise counts by industry code and
  employee band. Codes: **68.32** property management, **69.20** accounting and audit,
  **66.22** insurance agents and brokers. Ask for the 10-19 and 20-49 bands. FSR's
  Brancheanalyse is a Power BI dashboard holding the audit-firm size distribution.
- **Lithuania:** osp.stat.gov.lt publishes the same structure. Lithuanian accounting and
  property firm counts are genuinely unknown from this research.

Perhaps two hours of work, and it would firm up section 10 considerably.

### 14.5 Research the tooling could not reach

Stated plainly so nobody assumes it was covered:

- **Reddit was blocked to three of the four research streams.** The property stream got in
  via RSS, which is why the property verbatims are so much richer than the other two. **The
  accounting and insurance practitioner vocabulary is under-sampled**, and the insurance
  drudgery register in particular ("cert monkey", "glorified certificate machine") is
  reportedly real but **could not be retrieved, so it must not be quoted.**
- **G2, Gartner, AccountingWEB and Glassdoor** returned 403 throughout. Several AccountingWEB
  quotes are snippet-sourced and should be re-verified before being used as pull quotes.
- **Danish practitioner emotion is thin.** Danish administrators and auditors do not vent on
  public forums the way Anglophone ones do. Everything Danish here comes from job ads, trade
  guides, FAQ pages and register data: high credibility, low emotion.

> **This is the one gap that cannot be closed by better searching.** Plan **8 to 12
> discovery interviews in Copenhagen as a research deliverable**, not just a sales activity.
> The Danish emotional language for this product does not exist in public and has to be
